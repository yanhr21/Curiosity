Human-like robot learning  ·  NVIDIA Research  ·  project brief · 2026·07

Robot Baby

Teach a robot the way a child learns: watch a third-person video, then figure out the rest by doing. No teleoperation, no hand-labeled 2D/3D trajectories, no per-object 3D reconstruction, no physically-solved contact. Just two signals — demonstration and reinforcement — the same two that carry almost all of human and animal skill acquisition.

The pitch — in one paragraph
Today's robot-learning data pipeline is too expensive: it wants accurate 2D/3D trajectories, dense 3D reconstruction, and physically-correct contact and forces before a policy can learn anything. Humans need none of that. Hands-on teaching is rare for people and almost never how animals learn — they rely on (1) demonstration: imitate what a capable adult is doing, and (2) reinforcement: adjust from the actual situation until they reach a similar goal. Robot Baby builds exactly this: a robot that imitates a third-person demonstration video and reaches similar task success through RL in a physically-rich simulator — for vision-tactile manipulation.
Two parts · one framework
Part 1 — SDG engine (Shengze). A Newton / Isaac Sim-based synthetic-data-generation engine: physically-enriched 3D assets arranged like real human environments (via REST3D-style simulation-ready reconstruction), giving demonstration-conditioned RL an unlimited, contact-rich playground.  Part 2 — Demonstration-conditioned RL (Hongru & Shengze). A vision-tactile policy conditioned on a third-person demo video that closes the gap to the demonstrated goal through reinforcement — improving on imperfect demos and coping with the human→robot embodiment gap.

01

The motivation

Why the current pipeline is the wrong cost curve — and what nature does instead.

The expensive status quo. To make a robot learn a manipulation skill from human video today, the community first turns the video into something a controller can consume: accurate 2D and 3D trajectories, often a dense 3D reconstruction of hand and objects, then a physically-correct pass for contact, forces, and stability so the retargeted motion is executable. Each of these stages is fragile, compute-heavy, and object-specific — and the resulting policy quality is capped by the weakest link in the recon chain. Teleoperation avoids recon but replaces it with an even scarcer resource: human hands-on time, one skill at a time.

Nature disagrees. A child does not receive labeled trajectories, and no one solves contact forces for them. Hands-on teaching — physically guiding a learner's limbs — is rare for humans and almost absent in animals. Skill instead flows through two channels: demonstration (watch a capable adult and imitate the intent) and reinforcement (try it, feel the outcome, adjust toward a similar goal). The demonstration is third-person and approximate; the correction comes from acting in the world, not from a pre-solved plan.

Robot Baby's bet: if two signals — third-person demonstration and reinforcement — are enough for a child, they are enough for a robot. Everything the expensive pipeline computes (trajectories, recon, contact) is either unnecessary or something the robot should discover through its own interaction in a rich enough simulator.

What we deliberately drop — and what we keep

Drop — the curation tax

No recon, no teleop, no solved contact

  • No accurate 2D/3D hand-object trajectory extraction.
  • No per-object dense 3D reconstruction of the demo.
  • No physically-correct contact/force solving of the demo motion.
  • No teleoperated, action-labeled data collection.
  • No per-task hand-engineered dense reward.
Keep — the two cheap signals

Third-person demonstration + reinforcement

  • Demonstration: an approximate third-person video of the goal being achieved.
  • Reinforcement: the robot acts, observes vision+tactile outcome, and adjusts.
  • Contact is learned, not solved — discovered through interaction, sensed by touch.
  • Reward = similarity to the demonstrated outcome, not a bespoke reward.
02

The framework

Two parts that feed each other: a synthetic-data engine that manufactures the world, and a demonstration-conditioned RL learner that lives in it.

Part 1 · SDG engine

Newton / Isaac Sim world factory

owner: Shengze

A synthetic-data-generation engine that builds physically-enriched 3D scenes arranged like real human environments — kitchens, desks, workbenches — where every object is simulation-ready and contact-rich.

Assets: REST3D-style reconstruction turns casual images into physically-stable, penetration-free, sim-ready 3D assets, then arranges them into human-plausible layouts.

Physics: Newton (GPU physics on Warp + OpenUSD) and Isaac Sim / Isaac Lab give massively-parallel, contact-accurate rollouts — the playground where reinforcement actually happens.

Output: unlimited randomized episodes with vision + tactile signals, ready for Part 2.

Part 2 · the learner

Demonstration-conditioned RL

owners: Hongru & Shengze

A vision-tactile policy conditioned on a third-person demo video. The demo sets the goal; reinforcement in Part-1's world closes the gap to it.

Conditioning: encode the demo into a viewpoint- and embodiment-invariant goal/intent the policy consumes at every step.

Learning: RL improves on suboptimal demos, absorbs the human→robot embodiment gap, and learns contact through touch rather than from a solved plan.

Reward: similarity to the demonstrated outcome — video/goal-based, not hand-shaped per task.

Every reference column pays a tax we refuse: teleop pays in human hours, video-recon pays in reconstruction and contact-solving, pure RL pays in reward engineering. Prior demo-conditioned RL has the right idea but stops at state demos and simple embodiments. Robot Baby keeps one third-person video + reinforcement, and pushes the idea to vision-tactile manipulation in human-arranged worlds.

The roadmap

Where we are (bring-up) and the ordered path to a first demonstration-conditioned, vision-tactile RL result. The living version is TODOs.md in the Docs tab.

Part 1 — SDG engine (Shengze)

  1. Stand up Newton + Isaac Lab on the cluster; parallel-rollout smoke test with vision + tactile sensors wired.
  2. REST3D asset path: reconstruct a first batch of physically-stable, sim-ready assets and validate no float/penetration under simulation.
  3. Human-arranged scene sampler: procedurally place REST3D assets into plausible human layouts (kitchen/desk/workbench) with randomization.
  4. Episode + sensor API: emit randomized episodes carrying synchronized RGB, depth, proprioception, and tactile streams for Part 2.

Part 2 — demonstration-conditioned RL (Hongru & Shengze)

  1. Demo encoder: choose/adapt a viewpoint- & embodiment-invariant video representation for third-person conditioning (survey in Related Work).
  2. Reward from demo: define demo-similarity reward (goal/video-based) that needs no per-task shaping.
  3. Demo-conditioned policy: π(a | vision, tactile, demo) trained with RL in the Part-1 world; verify it beats a demo-free RL baseline and improves on suboptimal demos.
  4. Embodiment gap: quantify human→robot transfer; ablate tactile vs. vision-only on contact-rich tasks.
  5. Sim-to-real: domain-randomized transfer of a first skill to hardware.

Story milestones (for the community & NVIDIA)

  1. M1: "one third-person video → sim task success" on a single contact-rich task, no recon anywhere in the loop.
  2. M2: tactile ablation showing touch closes the contact gap that vision-only imitation cannot.
  3. M3: generalization across REST3D-arranged scenes from a single demo.
  4. M4: sim-to-real transfer of the demonstration-conditioned policy.
